A skin is a whole look for your dashboards: how every light, sensor and lock is drawn, on a phone and a wall panel, in
Home Assistant's light and dark modes. You don't need to write code to have one of your own. Show an AI coding agent a
picture you like, or tell it what you want in a few words. It builds the skin, shows it to you, changes it until it's
right and puts it on your Home Assistant.

It works because the hard part is already done. Every card already talks to Home Assistant, and the skin comes with
checks that keep it working on a phone and in dark mode, plus a guide written for agents. The agent only changes how
things look.

<Aside type="caution" title="Before 1.0">
  What a skin builds on becomes a fixed contract at 1.0; until then a minor version may still change it, and its
  release says how. A new skin stays on the `0.x` minor it started from until you move it.
  [More](/dashhaus/intro/#before-10).
</Aside>

## Ask an agent to build it

### What you need

- A computer: Mac, Windows or Linux. The agent works there, not inside Home Assistant.
- An AI coding agent that can run commands on that computer, such as Claude Code.
- A picture of a look you like (a screenshot, a photo of a device or a room, a poster), or a few words about it.
- For the last step only: an administrator account in Home Assistant.

You don't need to install anything else yourself. The agent installs what it needs and tells you what it's doing.

<Steps>

1. Make an empty folder for your skin, for example **My dashboard** in your Documents, and open it in your agent.

2. Paste the prompt below. Replace the two lines in angle brackets with your own, and attach your picture if you have
   one.

   ```text title="Prompt" wrap
   Build me my own Home Assistant dashboard skin with Dashhaus. I'm not a developer: explain each step in plain words.

   Name: <a short name in lower case, like cosy-cabin>
   Look: <a few words about the look, or "like the picture I attached">

   1. In this folder, run `npm create dashhaus-skin@latest` with that name, then follow the AGENTS.md it writes. Install anything it needs first, such as Node.js 22 or newer, and tell me what you install.
   2. Restyle the skin to the look: palette, type, page and cards, at phone and desktop width, in light and dark mode. Keep `npm test` passing.
   3. Show me the snapshots, and change the skin until I say I like it.
   4. When I say I'm ready, deploy it with `npm run deploy`. Ask me for my Home Assistant's address, walk me through creating a long-lived access token, and help me set up the Terminal & SSH add-on so you can copy files into /config/www: make an SSH key if there is none and give me only its public line to paste. Keep the token in .env.
   ```

3. Look at what it shows you: pictures of your skin on a sample house, on a phone and a desktop, light and dark. Say
   what you'd change in your own words: "warmer colours", "bigger numbers", "rounder buttons", "more like the
   picture". Repeat until you like it.

4. Tell the agent you're ready to put it on Home Assistant. It asks for three things and walks you through each:

   - **Your Home Assistant's address**, the one in your browser when you open it, such as
     `http://homeassistant.local:8123`.
   - **A long-lived access token.** In Home Assistant, open your profile (your name at the bottom of the sidebar), then
     **Security**. Under **Long-lived access tokens**, press **Create token**, call it `Dashhaus` and give the agent
     what it shows. It's shown only once.
   - **A way to copy the skin into Home Assistant.** Open **Settings → Add-ons → Add-on store**, install
     **Terminal & SSH**, and in its **Configuration** tab paste the line the agent gives you into
     **Authorized keys**. Under **Network**, set the port to `22`. Save, then press **Start** on its **Info** tab.

   The agent keeps the token in a file on your computer, `.env`, which never goes into the skin.

5. Reload Home Assistant in your browser and open your skin's dashboard from the sidebar. It shows every light and
   room sensor you have, grouped by area, and every other kind of entity the agent added a card for.

</Steps>

### Change it later

Open the same folder in your agent and say what you want, then ask it to deploy again. Your dashboard's layout is kept.

- "Add my blinds and my thermostat": it adds a card for any kind of entity on the
  [Entities](/dashhaus/entities/) pages.
- "Show the brightness on the light card", "make the alarm card stand out".
- "Put a greeting and the date at the top of the page."

To see what agents make, open [Studies](/dashhaus/develop/studies/): skins agents built, kept so you can take ideas from
one or start from it.

<Aside title="Orb and Field too?">
  Your skin doesn't replace them. You can [install Orb and Field](/dashhaus/start/install/) on the same Home
  Assistant, before or after, and switch between all three from the sidebar.
</Aside>

## Build it yourself

The rest of this page is what the agent does, for you to do in a terminal or to follow along. `create-dashhaus-skin`
writes a working skin in a new folder, with a light you can switch and a room sensor you can read, both with details
that open from the card, on the real hooks. `npm run add` draws any other domain the same way. You change the drawing;
the Home Assistant side is already done. It starts from the same templates as the skins built inside the Dashhaus
repository. It also writes an `AGENTS.md`, the guide your agent follows: where the colours, type, parts and rules live,
and how to restyle the skin from a screenshot.

CI runs these steps on every change to Dashhaus: it packs the release, installs `@dashhaus/core`,
`@dashhaus/dashboard`, `@dashhaus/deploy` and the scaffolder into an empty folder, scaffolds `my-skin`, runs its
checks, builds it and deploys it into a simulated Home Assistant.

<Steps>

1. Scaffold the skin. The folder's name is the skin's: lower-case kebab-case, new or empty.

   ```sh
   npm create dashhaus-skin@latest my-skin
   cd my-skin
   npm install
   ```

   From the release tarballs, in the folder that holds them:

   ```sh
   npx --yes --package ./create-dashhaus-skin-0.2.1.tgz create-dashhaus-skin my-skin --tarballs .
   cd my-skin
   npm install
   ```

   `--tarballs` points the `@dashhaus` dependencies at the tarballs instead of the registry; `--dry-run` lists the
   files instead of writing them. `npm install` brings `@dashhaus/core` and `@dashhaus/dashboard`, React and
   `home-assistant-js-websocket` as peers, and TypeScript, Vite and `@dashhaus/deploy` for the checks, the build and
   the deploy.

2. Look at it. The scaffold draws the real element on a sample house that already holds the worst cases: an
   unavailable light, a sensor with no reading and a long name.

   ```sh
   npm run preview     # in the browser, at the URL it prints
   npm run snapshot    # or as PNGs in preview/snapshots/
   ```

   Both use the same shadow root and CSS as Home Assistant. The first snapshot prints the command that installs
   Playwright's Chromium if it's missing. Snapshot again after every change and compare with your reference.

3. Check the cards:

   ```sh
   npm test
   ```

   It typechecks the skin, then runs the conformance check from `@dashhaus/dashboard/conformance` over `cards/` and
   the CSS rules: each card has its component and its stories under the skin's title and classes under the skin's
   prefix, no presentational card imports the Home Assistant layer, every colour is a token,
   `:hover` sits under the hover media query and motion avoids layout properties. Each finding names its file, line
   and fix. Run it after every card change.

   ```text
   conformance: 2 cards and 6 stylesheets, no findings
   ```

4. Build one file:

   ```sh
   npm run build
   ```

   `dist/dashhaus-my-skin.js` is one ES module carrying React, the cards and their CSS: Home Assistant loads one
   resource and needs no stylesheet. Its name is the skin's, so it lives beside the prebuilt bundle on one Home
   Assistant.

5. Deploy it. Copy the scaffold's `.env.example` to `.env` and fill in `HA_URL`, `HA_TOKEN` and `HA_WWW` as
   [Deploy from the command line](/dashhaus/start/deploy/) describes, then:

   ```sh
   npm run deploy
   ```

   It builds, copies the bundle into Home Assistant, registers it as a resource and creates the **Home (My Skin)**
   dashboard the first time, in a panel view. The last step fetches the file back from Home Assistant, so a wrong
   `HA_WWW` shows up here instead of as a blank dashboard. `npx dashhaus-deploy --help` prints the options, a config
   to start from and the `.env` keys.

   <Example template="dashboard.config.mjs" />

</Steps>

### A card and its connection

A card takes its domain's normalized model as `entity`, today's `history`, its footprint, and one callback per action
it shows. It draws inside the skin's card frame, so its shape, its name that opens its details and the details dialog
are the frame's, and it never imports the Home Assistant layer: a card can be drawn with any data. The neutral card
shows the name, the state, one headline reading and its actions; the model has every other fact the hook reads, typed
in `@dashhaus/core/entities/<domain>/types`.

<Example template="cards/light/MySkinLightCard.tsx" />

The adapter in `ha/` is the one place the card meets Home Assistant: the shared hook above it, passing its model and
actions to the card.

<Example template="ha/HaMySkinLightCard.tsx" />

The shared parts are the card frame, on `useCardFrame`, `detailsSheet` and `detailsTile` from
`@dashhaus/dashboard/card`, which names each card's article after its title and opens the details in a `CardDialog`
(open, close, focus and the page's scroll lock), and the action, on the `usePress` primitive (press and hold,
`aria-pressed`, an unavailable state that stays focusable).

### The Lovelace card

`registry.tsx` routes an entity to its card by its Home Assistant domain. `config.ts` reads the YAML through the shared
`Reader` from `@dashhaus/dashboard/schema`, so every mistake is collected with its path and a suggestion, as in Orb
and Field; its card kinds are the registry's.

`card.tsx` is the element, on `LovelaceDashboardElement` from `@dashhaus/dashboard/skin`: it builds the store on Home
Assistant's own connection, puts the CSS in its shadow root and draws the page. `paintHost` puts Home Assistant's dark
mode on the host as `data-my-skin-theme`, the attribute `styles/tokens.css` reads: the skin's own, so another skin on
the same page never re-colours yours. The skin's web font is `MY_SKIN_FONT_HREF` in `shared/surface.ts`. The page ends
with `MySkinActionStatus`, the row that says when Home Assistant refused a command, with Retry and Dismiss. Without
`cards` in the YAML, `auto.ts` lays out every entity a card is registered for, by area, with
`@dashhaus/dashboard/layout`.

## Add a domain

A cover, in one command. Any entity on the [Entities](/dashhaus/entities/) pages goes the same way.

```sh
npm run add -- cover
```

It writes the card in `cards/cover/` with its stylesheet, glyph and story metadata, and its adapter on `useHaCover`.
A cover is a control, so its actions are its buttons; `--kind reading` draws a domain without them. An action that
asks first, as a lock's unlock does, is never one tap: `npm run add -- lock` draws its button to open the details on
the question, or on the code keypad when the lock wants a code. An action that takes a value, a brightness or an
alarm's mode, is left out for your own control, and the command says so.

<Example template="cards/cover/MySkinCoverCard.tsx" added />

It registers the card for Home Assistant's `cover` domain, so the YAML and the automatic layout pick it up, and the
preview already has a cover on its demo house.

<Example template="lovelace/registry.tsx" added ins={["HaMySkinCoverCard"]} />

Then `npm test`, `npm run snapshot`, and give it its own drawing. `npm run build` and `npm run deploy` put it in Home
Assistant.

## The rules a skin keeps

- One card, one model. A card draws one entity's model, or that entity plus the companions its hook composes (a
  temperature sensor with the humidity sensor on its device), handed to it by its adapter, never from Home Assistant.
- A card may import `@dashhaus/core`'s entity models, primitives and formatters, and `@dashhaus/dashboard/card`; never
  `@dashhaus/core` itself, `@dashhaus/core/websocket`, its demo or testing subpaths, or `@dashhaus/dashboard`.
  `npm test` enforces it.
- Every colour comes from `styles/tokens.css`, under the skin's prefix, so another skin on the same page can't collide
  with it.
- Dark mode keys on the skin's own attribute, `data-my-skin-theme`, never the shared `data-theme` another skin on the
  page may set. `npm test` enforces it.
- A skin never imports another skin.

The scaffold's `AGENTS.md` repeats these beside the files they govern, with the rest of the look's rules.

[Packages](/dashhaus/packages/#for-a-skin-author) lists what each `@dashhaus/dashboard` subpath promises a skin.

For a skin drawn end to end with the generators, to take ideas from or build on, see [Studies](/dashhaus/develop/studies/).

## Orb and Field beside your skin

Your skin's bundle carries your skin only. To offer Orb and Field on the same Home Assistant, install
`@dashhaus/bundle` too and deploy it as on [Deploy from the command line](/dashhaus/start/deploy/): it uploads to its own
folder, so the two bundles don't overwrite each other.
